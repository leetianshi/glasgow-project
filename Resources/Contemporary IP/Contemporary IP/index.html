    2. Exam Preparation

    2.1 Seminar 1

    [Chunck 010101]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; noncopyright interests; market exclusivity; economic incentives; legal strategy
    Essay-ready point: Copyright weaponization occurs when copyright is invoked not to protect market exclusivity or economic incentives, but to advance noncopyright interests such as suppression, retaliation, reputation management, or privacy protection.
    Original Sentence: All these stories have one thing in common: they all involve copyright owners using copyright to advance noncopyright interests. This Article refers to these actions as ‘weaponizing copyright.’
    Page: 196

    [Chunck 010102]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; utilitarian copyright; incentives; dissemination; market exclusivity; public benefit
    Essay-ready point: The article contrasts weaponized copyright with copyright’s conventional justification: copyright protects authors’ market exclusivity so that economic incentives will encourage socially valuable creation.
    Original Sentence: Copyright grants authors exclusive rights in their works in order to encourage creation and dissemination of socially valuable works.
    Page: 195

    [Chunck 010103]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; economic interest; licensing market; copyright purpose; misuse; non-economic claims
    Essay-ready point: A central critique is that weaponized copyright does not serve copyright’s traditional function because the claimant is often not trying to preserve a licensing market or recover economic loss.
    Original Sentence: In none of these stories are individuals asserting copyright to protect their market exclusivity or economic interests — the intended purposes of copyright.
    Page: 197

    [Chunck 010104]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; silencing; erasure; retaliation; reputation; privacy
    Essay-ready point: Smith identifies five recurring noncopyright objectives: erasing facts, suppressing criticism, punishing perceived wrongdoing, protecting reputation or moral rights, and preserving privacy.
    Original Sentence: The article identifies “five common noncopyright objectives”: “to silence and erase facts, suppress criticism and speech, punish and retaliate, protect reputation and moral rights, and preserve privacy.”
    Page: 197

    [Chunck 010105]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; bad faith; vulnerable parties; defensive copyright; power imbalance; nuance
    Essay-ready point: A nuanced argument in the article is that non-economic uses of copyright should not automatically be treated as bad faith, because copyright may sometimes be used defensively by vulnerable people.
    Original Sentence: The first is that not all weaponized or noncopyright uses of copyright are bad, and the second is that not all typical copyright uses are good.
    Page: 197

    [Chunck 010106]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; power dynamics; aggressor; vulnerable parties; legal weapon; defensive use
    Essay-ready point: Smith frames copyright as a legal weapon whose moral significance depends partly on who uses it: it may further domination when used by the powerful, but provide protection when used by the vulnerable.
    Original Sentence: A weapon can be in the hand of an aggressor to cause harm… or it can be in the hand of the vulnerable to even the playing field.
    Page: 197

    [Chunck 010107]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; retaliation; punishment; spite; property rights; copyright abuse
    Essay-ready point: When copyright is used primarily to punish or retaliate, it resembles a spite-based assertion of property rights rather than an attempt to protect creative incentives.
    Original Sentence: In each of these instances, copyright is weaponized to punish and retaliate.
    Page: 201

    [Chunck 010108]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; DMCA strike; PewDiePie; racist speech; sexist speech; retaliation
    Essay-ready point: The Campo Santo and Alinity examples show copyright strikes being used not to prevent market substitution, but to punish racist or sexist speech by a popular online figure.
    Original Sentence: Neither asserted copyright against PewDiePie to protect the market exclusivity or economic interests in their works. Instead, the primary objective ... was to punish PewDiePie.
    Page: 203

    [Chunck 010109]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; injunction; chilling effect; litigation delay; suppression; TD Bank
    Essay-ready point: Even unsuccessful copyright weaponization may cause real harm because litigation and injunctions can suppress speech or distribution for extended periods before the claim is resolved.
    Original Sentence: TD Bank was successful in using copyright to suppress the dissemination of an author’s book for years.
    Page: 204

    [Chunck 010110]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; information control; erasure; public knowledge; concealment; historical record
    Essay-ready point: Another form of weaponization occurs when copyright owners seek not to exploit their works, but to prevent public access to embarrassing, damaging, or politically inconvenient information.
    Original Sentence: Copyright is also weaponized to erase facts and bury information.
    Page: 207

    [Chunck 010111]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; investigative journalism; Harvey Weinstein; Ronan Farrow; silencing victims; public interest
    Essay-ready point: The attempted use of copyright against Ronan Farrow illustrates how copyright threats may be deployed to obstruct journalism and silence victims, rather than to vindicate any economic copyright interest.
    Original Sentence: The copyright threats ... were not based on a market or financial interest ... but were an attempt to silence Weinstein’s victims.
    Page: 200

    [Chunck 010112]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; memory holes; historical record; truth; public discourse; censorship
    Essay-ready point: Smith, drawing on Goldman and Silbey, argues that copyright can create social “memory holes” by removing facts and ideas from public circulation, thereby distorting collective memory and the search for truth.
    Original Sentence: Asserting copyright to erase facts and bury information can create ‘memory holes’ in society.
    Page: 213–214

    [Chunck 010113]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; reputation; moral rights; dignity; association; expressive control
    Essay-ready point: Copyright may also be weaponized to protect reputation, dignity, or moral objections, especially where authors object to their works being associated with political, racist, or controversial messages.
    Original Sentence: Copyright is also weaponized to protect reputation, moral rights, and other dignitary interests.
    Page: 219

    [Chunck 010114]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; privacy; revenge porn; intimate images; personal autonomy; defensive copyright
    Essay-ready point: The article treats privacy-based copyright claims as especially difficult because they may protect legitimate personal interests, such as removing intimate images from the internet, even though they remain outside copyright’s economic rationale.
    Original Sentence: Copyright is also weaponized to preserve privacy. Women use copyright to remove intimate photographs of themselves from the Internet.
    Page: 223

    [Chunck 010115]
    Key words: copyright weaponization; copyright reform; legal gaps; vulnerable parties; privacy law; balanced framework
    Essay-ready point: Smith’s conclusion is that reform cannot simply condemn all copyright weaponization, because doing so might suppress harmful abuses but also remove one of the few available tools for vulnerable people seeking privacy, dignity, or protection.
    Original Sentence: Reforms ... need to be concurrent with reforms that strengthen laws or fill gaps that individuals are currently weaponizing copyright to fill.
    Page: 265

    2.2 Seminar 2

    [Chunck 020201]
    Key words: online copyright; everyday internet use; streaming; downloading; hyperlinking
    Essay-ready point: A useful starting point is that EU copyright law reaches many ordinary internet activities, including streaming, downloading, and even hyperlinking, because online copying and online dissemination may both trigger exclusive rights.
    Original Sentence: Activities like streaming music and films, downloading an e-book or news article, or even posting a hyperlink may, and often do, have copyright significance.
    Page: 386

    [Chunck 020202]
    Key words: reproduction right; communication to the public; dissemination; EU copyright; exclusive rights
    Essay-ready point: In EU copyright law, online use is mainly structured around two exclusive rights: reproduction for copying protected content, and communication to the public for online dissemination.
    Original Sentence: Copying of protected content ... is subject to the right of reproduction. Online dissemination of that content to the general internet public is subject to the right of communication to the public.
    Page: 386

    [Chunck 020203]
    Key words: Article 3 InfoSoc; CJEU; legal uncertainty; communication to the public; EU harmonisation
    Essay-ready point: The right of communication to the public, harmonised in Article 3 of the InfoSoc Directive, is one of the most contested areas of EU copyright law because CJEU case law has produced complex and uncertain criteria.
    Original Sentence: The right is harmonized in Article 3 of Directive 2001/29/EC (InfoSoc Directive) and its interpretation is perhaps the most controversial aspect of EU copyright law.
    Page: 386

    [Chunck 020204]
    Key words: WCT; WPPT; making available; international copyright; digital environment
    Essay-ready point: The EU right of communication to the public builds on international copyright instruments, especially the WIPO treaties, which adapted communication rights to digital and on-demand environments.
    Original Sentence: The WCT includes a general exclusive right of communication to the public in Article 8. Both the WCT and WPPT include ... an exclusive right of ‘making available.’
    Page: 387

    [Chunck 020205]
    Key words: making available; access; online communication; liability; on-demand services
    Essay-ready point: For online communication, liability may arise from making a work available to the public even if no member of the public actually accesses or receives the work.
    Original Sentence: The ‘making available’ prong ... does not require simultaneous reception of the work by the public and is independent of whether, where, and how often the work is accessed.
    Page: 387

    [Chunck 020206]
    Key words: autonomous concept; EU law; consistent interpretation; InfoSoc Directive; Rental and Lending Directive
    Essay-ready point: Because “communication to the public” is treated as an autonomous concept of EU law, it should be interpreted consistently across relevant EU copyright instruments.
    Original Sentence: ‘Communication to the public’ is an autonomous concept of EU law.
    Page: 388

    [Chunck 020207]
    Key words: cumulative conditions; act of communication; public; CJEU framework; Article 3
    Essay-ready point: The CJEU’s framework requires two essential cumulative conditions: there must be an act of communication, and that act must be directed to a public.
    Original Sentence: The first two main conditions are essential and cumulative ... The first cumulative condition is the existence of an ‘act of communication’ ... The second cumulative condition is that the communication is made to the ‘public.’
    Page: 388

    [Chunck 020208]
    Key words: intervention; knowledge; deliberate act; access; protected work
    Essay-ready point: A communication normally requires a deliberate intervention by the user, meaning that the user knowingly facilitates access to the protected work.
    Original Sentence: The user intervenes ‘in full knowledge of the consequences of his action, to give his customers access to a protected work.’
    Page: 389

    [Chunck 020209]
    Key words: indispensable intervention; Filmspeler; Ziggo; access facilitation; broad liability
    Essay-ready point: In online cases, the Court has moved away from a strict requirement that the intervention be indispensable, allowing liability where the defendant’s role merely makes access significantly easier.
    Original Sentence: In these cases, the intervention does not need to be indispensable. It suffices that it is very important.
    Page: 389

    [Chunck 020210]
    Key words: primary liability; broad interpretation; intervention; exclusive right; online platforms
    Essay-ready point: By treating important facilitation as an act of communication, the CJEU broadens the range of online conduct capable of triggering primary copyright liability.
    Original Sentence: It surely allows a broader range of acts to qualify as an intervention, thereby broadening the range of activities that may fall within the scope of the exclusive right and give rise to primary liability.
    Page: 390

    [Chunck 020211]
    Key words: public; indeterminate audience; de minimis threshold; private communication; recipients
    Essay-ready point: The “public” requirement excludes purely private communications and requires an indeterminate, fairly large group of potential recipients.
    Original Sentence: The public is defined as an ‘indeterminate’ and ‘fairly large number’ of recipients as potential beneficiaries of the communication.
    Page: 391

    [Chunck 020212]
    Key words: new public; hyperlinking; authorisation; right holder; Svensson
    Essay-ready point: Where the same technical means are used, a communication may still infringe if it reaches a “new public,” meaning a public not considered by the right holder when authorising the initial communication.
    Original Sentence: A work is communicated to a ‘new public’ ... a public not taken into account by the copyright holder when they authorized the initial communication.
    Page: 392

    [Chunck 020213]
    Key words: Svensson; hyperlinking; freely accessible content; new public; authorisation
    Essay-ready point: In Svensson, the CJEU treated hyperlinking as an act of making available, but held that links to freely accessible authorised content do not require a new authorisation because they do not reach a new public.
    Original Sentence: The CJEU first considered that the provision of direct access to the works via a link from one website to another is an act of making available ... However, to trigger Article 3(1) InfoSoc Directive, the acts ‘must also be directed at a new public.’
    Page: 392

    [Chunck 020214]
    Key words: GS Media; knowledge presumption; profit-making; unauthorised content; hyperlinking
    Essay-ready point: After GS Media, the legality of linking to unauthorised content depends heavily on knowledge, with commercial linkers facing a presumption that they knew the linked content was unauthorised.
    Original Sentence: After GS Media, it is arguable that [profit-making nature] plays a decisive role to establish a presumption of knowledge ... in scenarios of hyperlinking to works available online without the consent of the rights holder.
    Page: 398

    [Chunck 020215]
    Key words: safe harbours; value gap; press publishers; platform liability; copyright reform
    Essay-ready point: Quintais argues that reform should not expand communication-to-the-public liability through press-publisher rights or platform filtering obligations; instead, it should clarify the right while preserving hosting safe harbours and avoiding conflicts with fundamental rights.
    Original Sentence: The article argues for abandoning the legislative proposals for a new right for press publishers and the so-called value gap ... Instead, reform should focus on redefining the right of communication to the public and preserving safe harbors, especially for hosting providers.
    Page: 385

    [Chunck 020301]
    Key words: server rule; server test; direct liability; hyperlinking; Perfect 10
    Essay-ready point: In U.S. law, the server rule generally prevents hyperlinking from constituting direct copyright infringement unless the defendant stores and serves the copyrighted content.
    Original Sentence: In the U.S., a judge-made doctrine known as the ‘server test’ or ‘server rule’ governs the treatment of hyperlinks under the Copyright Act.
    Page: 177

    [Chunck 020302]
    Key words: Perfect 10 v Amazon; Ninth Circuit; server rule; precedent; hyperlink liability
    Essay-ready point: The leading U.S. authority is Perfect 10 v. Amazon, where the Ninth Circuit adopted the server rule and influenced later courts’ treatment of hyperlinking.
    Original Sentence: The Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit adopted the test in a 2007 case, Perfect 10 v. Amazon, and several U.S. courts since then have followed it.
    Page: 177

    [Chunck 020303]
    Key words: hyperlinking; store and serve; server location; display right; distribution; in-line linking
    Essay-ready point: Under the server rule, the decisive question is not whether the user sees the work through the defendant’s website, but whether the defendant’s own server stores and transmits the work.
    Original Sentence: Under the ‘server rule,’ ‘the owner of a computer that does not store and serve ... electronic information to a user is not displaying [or distributing] that information, even if such owner in-line links to or frames the electronic information.’
    Page: 177

    [Chunck 020304]
    Key words: hyperlinking; simple linking; deep linking; framing; in-line linking; direct infringement
    Essay-ready point: The server rule applies broadly to simple links, deep links, framing, and in-line links, meaning that hyperlink providers usually avoid direct infringement liability if they do not host the content.
    Original Sentence: A defendant who provides a hyperlink of any kind (through simple linking, deep linking, framing, or in-line linking) cannot incur direct copyright liability unless that defendant also ‘store[s] and serve[s]’ the copyrighted material to which the link points.
    Page: 177

    [Chunck 020305]
    Key words: hyperlinking; Google Image Search; thumbnails; full-size images; third-party servers; fair use
    Essay-ready point: In Perfect 10, Google’s stored thumbnail images raised direct infringement issues, but full-size images shown through links did not, because those images were stored on third-party servers.
    Original Sentence: There was a fundamental distinction between the presentation of Google’s ‘thumbnail’ image copies (which were actually stored on Google servers) and the presentation of the full-sized images, which were not stored on Google servers but were instead served up to users through ‘framed’ or ‘in-line’ links.
    Page: 177–178

    [Chunck 020306]
    Key words: hyperlinking; HTML instructions; lines of text; display right; Ninth Circuit; technical formalism
    Essay-ready point: The Ninth Circuit’s reasoning rests on the view that a hyperlink merely provides HTML instructions and therefore is not equivalent to showing or displaying a copyrighted copy.
    Original Sentence: The Ninth Circuit contended that a link is, essentially ‘HTML instructions [constituting mere] lines of text,’ and that ‘[p]roviding these HTML instructions is not equivalent to showing a copy.’
    Page: 178

    [Chunck 020307]
    Key words: secondary liability; facilitation; direct infringement; contributory infringement; hyperlinking
    Essay-ready point: Because hyperlinking is generally excluded from direct infringement under the server rule, U.S. courts tend to treat links as facilitation of infringement rather than infringement itself.
    Original Sentence: U.S. courts instead characterize the provision of hyperlinks of any form as the facilitation of a user’s access to infringing works, which ‘raises only [secondary] liability issues.’
    Page: 179

    [Chunck 020308]
    Key words: hyperlinking; statutory breadth; device or process; transmission; public display; server rule critique
    Essay-ready point: Ginsburg and Budiardjo argue that the server rule narrows the Copyright Act despite statutory language that broadly covers transmission or communication by any device or process.
    Original Sentence: Despite the breadth and technological neutrality of those rights, the server rule effectively assumes that a hyperlink is not a ‘device or process’ that ‘transmit[s] or otherwise communicate[s] a performance or display of a work ... to the public.’
    Page: 179

    [Chunck 020309]
    Key words: hyperlinking; transmission process; clickable links; HTML code; browser retrieval; public display
    Essay-ready point: A strong critique of the server rule is that clickable hyperlinks do more than identify location: they initiate the technical process by which the user’s browser retrieves and displays the work.
    Original Sentence: The ‘process ... whereby images ... are received’ on a user’s screen certainly includes the hyperlinks and HTML code which cause that image to appear.
    Page: 179–180

    [Chunck 020310]
    Key words: hyperlinking; misplaced metaphors; library index; driving directions; trigger function; clickable links
    Essay-ready point: The article criticises analogies that treat hyperlinks as mere library indexes or driving directions, because clickable links also trigger the delivery of content to the user.
    Original Sentence: Hyperlinks do provide information about the online location of the content that the user seeks to access. This locational-information function is, indeed, analogous to the function fulfilled by a ‘library’s card index’ or ‘driving directions’ ... However, most hyperlinks are clickable ... and therefore these hyperlinks also act as a trigger.
    Page: 183

    [Chunck 020311]
    Key words: hyperlinking; deep links; in-line links; framing; direct access; contextual control
    Essay-ready point: Deep links and in-line links are more legally significant than simple references because they can deliver the content directly, bypass navigation, and frame the work within a new context.
    Original Sentence: Deep links and in-line links do more than provide locational information—they instead serve up content directly to the user.
    Page: 184

    [Chunck 020312]
